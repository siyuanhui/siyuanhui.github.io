*Written for ENGL2000J Academic Writing, UM-SJTU Joint Institute, Spring 2025.*

Chang-Rae Lee’s “Coming Home Again” and Alden Hayashi’s “Finding the Right Keiko” are both semi-autobiographical personal essays that explore how the narrators, both Asian Americans, cope with the loss of their mothers and reflect on their past family relationships. Through introspection on their parents’ cultural heritage, they transition from reckless children who cause regret in their relationships to responsible sons who acknowledge their regrets and attempt to remedy them through love. In both stories, the sons recognize the mental pressure exerted on their parents by the model minority stereotype. However, during their younger years, they exacerbate this pressure, leading to regret after their mothers’ deaths. Ultimately, by returning their mothers’ love and honoring their mothers’ worth, they find a way to alleviate this regret.

The model minority stereotype imposes mental pressure on Asian Americans. According to Pew Research Center, the model minority stereotype describes Asian Americans as “high-achieving economically and educationally, which has been attributed to Asians being hardworking and deferential to parental and authority figures” (PEW 49). Without external help, they “pull themselves up from hardship and discrimination to become a model of self-respect and achievement” (U.S. News & World Report magazine). By constructing this stereotype, white Americans create “an audience unsympathetic to the problems of Asian Americans, denying the existence of present-day discrimination against Asian Americans and the present-day effects of past discrimination” (Chang 1260). Furthermore, according to Bob Suzuki, the “over-anxious attempts by Asian Americans to gain acceptance” of American society through the model minority stereotype “have stripped them of their dignity and have caused severe psychological disorders characterized by lack of confidence, low self-esteem, excessive conformity and alienation” (Suzuki 1977). Japanese Americans are typical victims of this, as they experienced the “unjust” Japanese American internment, which was based on “racial prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership” (United States, Civil Liberties Act). As Alden Hayashi, a “Sansei, or third-generation Japanese American,” points out in his work “A Profound Silence,” his mother was “shipped from their home to a concentration camp,” and then “used as barter to secure the freedom of Americans incarcerated by Japan” (Hayashi). Later, Japanese Americans were constructed as the model minority, which necessitated the “suppression of Asian war memories” (Bak) and “shutting away parts of their identities that were not deemed acceptable” (A. Wu). This oppressive silence brought about a severe psychological toll on Japanese Americans, with many of them “[suffering] from severe PTSD” and “[drinking themselves] into unconsciousness” (Sakai, Unbroken Honor). According to the racial identity model, in order to show their resistance against the stereotype, minority groups tend to “[orient themselves] toward self-discovery of one’s own history and culture, an active seeking out of information and artifacts that enhance that person’s sense of identity and worth” (Sue & Sue, 1999). This trait appears in the works of both writers. While Chang-Rae Lee mainly explores the “questions of identity, exile and diaspora, assimilation, and alienation” (Y. Wu), Alden Hayashi states in his profile that his intention to write is “to honor the memories of [his] grandparents’ immigration to Hawaii, as well as to preserve the stories of the lives of their many descendants, the spirit and essence of [his] family” (Hayashi).

Firstly, in both stories, the sons recognize the hidden mental pressure behind the model minority stereotype. In “Coming Home Again,” the father epitomizes model minority success, while the mother internalizes this stereotype, leading to her “psychological disorders” (Suzuki 1977). The father’s “excellence as a student in medical school” and his “devotion to his work” (Lee 191) align with the stereotype of being “high-achieving educationally” and “hardworking” (PEW 49). By “working two times the American doctors”, he overcomes his “Asian face and imperfect English,” which reflects the model minority narrative of “pulling [himself] up from hardship and discrimination to become a model of self-respect and achievement” (U.S. News & World Report magazine). The mother strongly worships the model minority stereotype, as she “zealously recounts” the father’s excellence and reminds the narrator of his diligence “each night” (Lee 191). However, her internalization of the model minority stereotype has “stripped [her] of [her] dignity” and caused her to suffer from a “lack of confidence and low self-esteem” (Suzuki 1977). She sends the narrator to Exeter because she “worries [the writer’s character] is becoming too much like hers” (Lee 187). Her perception of herself as “sloth and vanity without outside pressure” (Lee 187) reflects her “anxiety and low self-esteem” (Suzuki 1977) under the model minority standard of being “self-driven and hardworking” (PEW 49). Her talent and past glory of the “all-Asia basketball championship” (Lee 190) do not fulfill the model minority stereotype of academic success like the father’s. Therefore, she “[considers] her own history to be immaterial,” “patently [diminishes] herself,” and “[finesses] a kind of self-removal by speaking of [his] father whenever she could” (Lee 191), illustrating how the model minority stereotype deprives her of “confidence, self-esteem, and dignity” (Suzuki 1977). This silence of one’s identity is similarly portrayed in the parents’ figures in the other story.

Similarly, in “Finding the Right Keiko,” the son recognizes the hidden emotional cost behind the model minority stereotype. The father represents model minority success. His intention to “prove their loyalty to the United States” in the context of Japanese Americans being “incarcerated in concentration camps” (Hayashi “Finding the Right Keiko”) aligns with the stereotype of “[deferring] to authority figures” (PEW 49). His deference makes him a “highly decorated” (Hayashi) veteran, and the “G.I. Bill” offers him the opportunity for “economic achievements” (Yenne), reflected in his “good, custom-made suits” (Hayashi). However, his success story is overshadowed by trauma. His “over-anxious attempts” to prove his loyalty and gain acceptance from the American authorities “strip [him] of dignity” and result in “many psychological disorders” (Suzuki 1977). He suffers from the terror of “the suicide mission of the 442nd Infantry Regiment” (Hayashi), where they were ordered to “risk their lives to rescue the lost battalion with the motto ‘Go For Broke’” (“442nd Infantry Regiment (United States)”), a mission that robbed them of their dignity and right to life. He is also tortured by the guilt that his high school buddies “[perished] while he alone survived to return to the United States, marry, and start a family” (Hayashi), a source of “alienation” (Suzuki 1977). The narrator always feels this pain “in [his father’s] deepest being,” like “an apricot pit stuck in his gut,” symbolizing that his father cannot digest or expel the painful memory but can only carry it with him (Hayashi). The narrator observes this when he catches his father “drinking sake in the backyard” at night, singing a tune in his “unsteady tenor” about life’s “crooked paths, painful regrets, and dashed ambitions.” Like many survivors who “[suffer] from severe PTSD,” he “drinks himself into unconsciousness” (Sakai, Unbroken Honor). In the morning, he claims to have “no recollection” of the painful song he sang the previous night, “shutting away parts of their identities that were not deemed acceptable” (Wu). This suggests that the narrative of success “[suppresses] Asian war memories,” silencing his sadness (Bak). It also reflects the real-life experience of the writer’s mother. As Hayashi recalls in his work “A Profound Silence,” she was “a hostage of the U.S. government, used as barter to secure the freedom of Americans incarcerated by Japan” and “took so many secrets to the grave with her” (Hayashi, “A Profound Silence”). She lost her human rights during the internment but remained silent about this painful memory throughout her life. In conclusion, through their parents, the narrators in both stories uncover the silenced emotional cost of the model minority stereotype.

After their mothers’ deaths, both narrators regret not alleviating their mothers’ mental pressure but exacerbating it. In “Coming Home Again,” the narrator acts as the authority figure in the mother-son relationship, denying his mother and intensifying her struggles with low self-esteem. In this dynamic, the mother sacrifices her individuality, anchoring her entire existence to her son by confessing that he is “her reason for living” and that she would wish for self-destruction if she “loses him in either body or spirit” (Lee 190), a sentiment that aligns with the “over-anxious attempt to gain acceptance” seen in many Asian Americans (Suzuki 1977). The son, like the American authority, takes his mother’s “deference” and emotional dependence for granted, creating “an audience unsympathetic” to her unconditional love and the mental toll it takes on her (Chang 1260). Before she even demonstrates her basketball skills, the narrator assumes her incapacity, “crying at her in exasperation” that she has gone in the wrong direction, which mirrors the underlying discrimination and bias of white Americans (Lee 191). When he grows “weary of her oft-repeated accounts of father’s success,” he “[dismisses] his home and family” and views his mother as the “object of [his] derision” (Lee 191). Each time he returns home from Exeter, she believes that “he finds her more and more ignorant,” further deepening her sense of inadequacy. When she asks him to call the bank, he dismisses her request “lightly,” saying “It’s just that you should be able to do it yourself” and emphasizes that she needs “practice” in Korean instead of English (Lee 191). In doing so, he directly highlights her perceived incapacity in communication, intensifying the emotional scars left by her low self-esteem. When the narrator rejects her prepared meal in favor of a picnic with a new student, she realizes she is losing him spiritually, which triggers her emotional collapse, mirroring the psychological disorders that Asian Americans experience when they fail to “gain acceptance” in American society (Suzuki 1977). Unfortunately, it is only near her death that the narrator begins to recognize her vulnerability. In the grief of losing her, he starts to regret “throttling” her with his indifference and denial and condemns himself for being an “obtuse, venal and spoiled son” (Lee 192). This regret is also present in the other story.

In “Finding the Right Keiko,” the narrator regrets intensifying the emotional scars of the model minority stereotype on his mother. In their relationship, he acts as the authority figure, much like American society, exacerbating her emotional pressure. For instance, his demand for a “homemade chocolate dobash,” an American dish completely unfamiliar to his mother, reflects the unjust orders placed upon Japanese Americans by white Americans during the internment. Her repeated attempts to make the birthday cake mirror her “over-anxious attempts to gain acceptance” (Suzuki 1977), and the resulting failure leads to her “frustration” (Hayashi). However, the son responds to her efforts with “the look of disappointment” (Hayashi), creating “an audience unsympathetic” to her unconditional love and the mental strain it causes her (Chang 1260). Moreover, every time she exasperatedly asks, “Are you deaf?” when offering advice, he “tunes out any advice of hers,” which corresponds to the indifference of white Americans who silence Asian Americans’ voices of request and protest. While she insists on her “mottainai” teachings, the narrator later “lives in a one-bedroom condo with just a small galley kitchen” and has no room for hoarding, instead donating his things to “Goodwill.” His divergence from her belief in “mottainai” reopens the “wound” left from the concentration camp, further intensifying her mental pressure (Hayashi). When the son loses her love after her death, he blames himself for being “an ungrateful, disrespectful son who isn’t appreciative for the parents’ love without reservation,” reflected in “the feast that my mother prepared” and “the Schwinn bicycle gift he longs for” (Hayashi). In conclusion, in both stories, the sons deeply regret exacerbating their mothers’ mental pressure by mimicking the indifferent attitude of white Americans towards them, failing to respond to their mothers’ love, and devaluing their worth.

Through following the mother’s cultural heritage, the narrators begin to respond to their mothers’ love and recognize their worth, which gradually alleviates their regret. In “Coming Home Again,” as his mother becomes seriously ill, the narrator takes on her role in the kitchen, preparing meals with “her favorite chef’s knife and old yellow apron” (Lee 187). Through cooking, the narrator “[orients himself] toward the discovery of his mother’s history and culture, seeking out information and artifacts that enhance the sense of identity and worth” of both his mother and himself (Sue & Sue, 1999). While recreating kalbi, he comes to understand her words more deeply: “The meat needs the bone nearby… to borrow its richness” (Lee 188). In this metaphor, the meat represents his individuality, while the bone symbolizes his mother’s support and the Korean culture she preserves, indicating that individual fulfillment draws its strength from familial love and cultural rootedness. Through this understanding, he grants his mother “identity and worth” (Sue & Sue, 1999), contrasting with his previous actions that lowered her self-esteem. Confronting the nearness of her death, the narrator realizes that he must now become that “bone” for the family, nurturing others as she once nurtured him. He prepares a final meal for her with “the finger dishes she fancies,” mirroring what she once lovingly prepared whenever he returned from Exeter (Lee 189). No longer the authority who criticizes her, he defers to her instruction, promising, “I’ll remember the salt next time” (Lee 192). Through this mutual agreement that “he can’t make it without her,” he rebuilds the emotional dependence his mother desires (Lee 192). By doing so, he truly becomes “her reason for living,” repairing the spiritual gap between them that had led to her collapse (Lee 191). When she hesitantly asks whether she had ever done anything he “wished she hadn’t,” he replies “no,” not merely to ease her doubt, but to affirm her love (Lee 192). He transitions from a boy who was “happily obtuse and venal to let his denying pain her” to a son who finally sees and cherishes her vulnerability (Lee 191). Even after her death, his response does not end. At the close of the story, the narrator envisions his parents through the rearview mirror, locked in an embrace as he “[drives] alone on the highway.” The road ahead stretches forward into time, but his mother remains behind, frozen in memory, unreachable. The mirror becomes a metaphor for his emotional state: he keeps moving forward, but never without looking back. His unanswered question, “Are they alright?” becomes, paradoxically, a form of reply. It offers no resolution, but in asking it, he carries her absence, her mental pressure, and her love with him as he moves on with his life. This act of remembrance becomes his way of loving her—late, imperfect, but lasting. Finally, though regret cannot be eliminated, he transforms it into love, a transformation that is also evident in “Finding the Right Keiko.”

In “Finding the Right Keiko”, through recollecting memories and cultural heritage, the narrator begins to understand his mother’s wisdom and respond to her love, which relieves his regret. At the request of the priest, he searches for the “kanji” for his mother’s name, “Keiko,” so that the priest can “arrive at her name for when she’s reborn” (Hayashi). Thus, he orients himself toward “a self-discovery of [his] own history and culture” (Sue & Sue, 1999). By the end of the story, he grasps the meaning of his mother’s love, recognizing how she tried to “open the door of understanding” and “let the light of wisdom stream through” (Hayashi), which “[enhances] the sense of identity and worth” of both his mother and himself (Sue & Sue, 1999). Through reflection, he begins to see her wisdom embedded in acts he once resisted and starts to respond to her love by embracing that wisdom. From her perseverance in attempting to fulfill his birthday cake wish, he learns the value of persistent devotion. Carrying this realization, he sifts through piles of belongings he had long avoided, persistently searching for his mother’s kanji to promise her spiritual “rebirth.” Upon reexamining her hoarding, he no longer sees it as a “scar” of Japanese internment, but as a form of wisdom—a deliberate act of preserving memory through objects. In the end, it is the tea set she hoarded that leads him to her name. As the narrator recalls memories of family love through small objects such as “sheep-hair brushes” and a “bamboo mah-jongg set,” he finally answers his mother’s intentions (Hayashi). Her insistence on Japanese school reflects her belief in language as a thread of cultural memory, a connection to his ancestors, community, and family. When he regrets that “he should have studied harder in Japanese school” and devotes himself to researching the kanji, he reclaims that cultural connection through the rediscovery of his parents’ recorded romance in America, thus finally responding to his mother’s love. Throughout this journey of remembrance, he understands her wisdom and affection, repaying her in the way she longed for. In offering this final response, he transforms guilt into love and finally forgives himself.

In conclusion, both “Coming Home Again” and “Finding the Right Keiko” trace the sons’ journeys from unknowingly reinforcing the emotional burdens of the model minority stereotype to consciously responding to their mothers’ love. In their youth, they recognize but unintentionally deepen the silent struggles their parents endure under model minority expectations. After their mothers’ deaths, they confront profound regret for failing to honor their mothers’ sacrifices and identities. Through reconnecting with cultural heritage and memory, they offer imperfect but lasting acts of love. These stories suggest that true healing in intergenerational relationships is not achieved by rejecting the past trauma but by understanding it, respecting it, and answering it with love, transforming inherited wounds into enduring connections.
