## The Hobbesian Question: Ruler, or Law?

This note starts from a Hobbesian question: how is a country actually built—through a ruler, or through law? At bottom it is a question of how power is distributed, and which political arrangement is the "fitter" one.

From a Darwinian point of view, this is survival of the fittest: different ways of allocating power are put side by side, and the one that adapts better to its environment survives.

Hobbes's logic runs as follows: everyone holds power, and left unchecked this slides into a war of all against all. To survive and secure peace, people must surrender their rights to one person or one body, forming the Leviathan. That body monopolizing violence is the prototype of the modern state.

## Why People Compete: Desire, Ability, and Resources

To understand why the state appears at all, start from human nature. A person has both a biological and a social dimension: the biological side serves survival, while the social side is explained differently by different philosophers—Maslow by the hierarchy of needs, Freud by libido, Nietzsche by the will to power, Russell by power and glory.

All these accounts circle the same thing: people have needs, which is to say desires. Desires can grow without limit; to satisfy them, people develop their abilities, and ability helps them acquire resources, which are finite. Desire, ability, and resources form a self-renewing triangle, and the contradiction sits right there.

Human nature itself has several readings: some see people as pure beasts; some as social beings shaped by society; some emphasize the moral person, guided by the Golden Rule—do not do to others what you would not want done to you; the more common view is the economic person, the self-interested individual described by Adam Smith in *The Wealth of Nations*, whose self-interest may ultimately also lead to altruism.

## Self-Interest Leads to Game Theory, in Two Directions

Start from the self-interested economic person. Their goal is to satisfy their own desires and gain the most satisfaction—utility maximization. For that they need to optimize: be rational enough, know the value of things, understand resource constraints, and rely on information to calculate. Information thereby becomes a crucial resource in modern society, and games split into complete-information and incomplete-information varieties.

When everyone pursues their own maximization, a game forms between them. Where self-interested game-playing leads depends on the view:

- One path, laid out in Axelrod's *The Evolution of Cooperation*, holds that self-interested competition eventually evolves into virtue and cooperation, exemplified by the Tit for Tat strategy;
- The other is Hobbes's older angle: like a dark forest, self-interest produces the war of everyone against everyone.

Following Hobbes's road leads to the Leviathan: a body must monopolize violence in exchange for peace for all. This is the well-known state paradox—the violence monopoly was meant to protect everyone, yet the body that holds the violence can itself turn against individuals.

## How the Machinery of Violence Is Sustained: Army, Taxation, Bureaucracy, and Surplus Output

The most direct sign of a violence monopoly is having an army: an external army to face enemies. But maintaining a standing force that trains and fights full-time requires money, which means taxation; if taxes cannot be collected, an internal bureaucracy is needed to enforce them.

A bureaucracy alone is not enough: society must produce a surplus. Total output must exceed the population's own consumption before soldiers and officials can be supported. Total output is roughly determined by population, productivity, and land resources: population differs from country to country, land resources depend on geography, and the most decisive factor is productivity.

Productivity is driven by technological revolution: the agricultural revolution raises output directly; metalworking strengthens the army; writing makes communication easier and gives the bureaucracy a push forward. Enriching the state largely serves strengthening its army.

The chain summarizes as: the force system gives rise to the tax system, and the tax system gives rise to the bureaucracy; what gives rise to the force system is war. War sets the modern state on a virtuous cycle that keeps consolidating it—"war made the state."

## Path One: Qin—a Prototype Modern State Forged in War

When did China first have a modern state? Qin can be counted as a prototype. The Spring-and-Autumn and Warring States periods began as a fief-based, feudal society in the Western sense, and grew into a modern state under the pressure of war. A modern state needs three elements—bureaucracy, monarchy, and centralization—and Qin had all of them.

During the Warring States period, power shifted from horizontal enfeoffment to vertical commandery-county administration. War pushed every state to strengthen its army, and military-merit ranks carried more weight; a bigger army required more taxation. Where did the tax come from? By stimulating farming, through land privatization: once people had an incentive, productivity rose. At the same time, to concentrate resources in the monarch's hands, the nobility's hold over people and land had to be weakened; the commandery-county system cut down the share of power carved out by enfeoffment.

## Path Two: European Feudalism and the Reshuffle by Cannon

The modern state formed much later in the West. The long Middle Ages were a feudal, fief-based society built on a contract between lord and vassal: the monarch granted land and protection to vassals or nobles, who returned loyalty and, when needed, military and financial aid.

Medieval warfare paired heavy cavalry on attack with castles on defense. Nobles lived in castles and sometimes commanded more heavy cavalry than the monarch, so noble power held the upper hand for a long time and royal power was weak. Yet to fight external wars, the central army had to be stronger; dispersed power means weak national strength abroad. Monarchs who wanted to tighten control mostly could not manage it in the Middle Ages.

Why did feudalism last so long? Its root lies in the chaos after Rome fell: in that raw, disordered landscape, the Frankish and Carolingian dynasties could not build a modern state directly. To raise their fighting capacity, monarchs had to recruit vassal nobles into a fief-based state; this armed class sustained feudalism through the whole medieval period.

The Middle Ages finally ended largely through one technological revolution: cannon. Cannon could smash castles outright and was far more effective than heavy cavalry, fundamentally undermining the nobility's military base. The monarch was the first to hold cannon, and thereby gained the power to dominate the nobles; aristocrats were weakened, power concentrated in the crown, and countries such as France formed absolutist modern states.

## Path Three: England—Nobles Rolling Back Royal Power

There was another way to form a modern state: England. The monarch likewise tried to curb the nobility, but failed; instead the nobles rolled back the monarch, draining royal power until parliamentary sovereignty emerged, producing an early capitalist state.

Why was the English crown so weak? Geopolitical pressure was low: France was the only serious enemy. England needed force to balance France, but the urge for centralization was never urgent.

Following English history along this line of bargaining:

- **House of Normandy (1066–1154)**: the Norman Conquest of 1066 brought William I the Conqueror (r. 1066–1087) to England, opening the era of continuous dynastic record;
- **Plantagenets (1154–1399)**: Richard I, the Lionheart (r. 1189–1199), campaigned on the Continent; John (r. 1199–1216) lost his continental possessions, creating the need for reconquest. By the earlier logic he had to centralize power and curb the nobles, but his attempt to break the lord-vassal contract failed; in 1215 he was forced to seal Magna Carta, the embryo of "king under the law." The law gave nobles a legitimate channel to voice their will and resist the crown;
- **The growth of Parliament (13th century)**: under Henry III (r. 1216–1272), the 1258 Oxford Provisions limited royal power, and the 1265 Simon de Montfort's Parliament first included commoner representatives; Edward I (r. 1272–1307) summoned the Model Parliament in 1295, with taxation and broader representation following the same logic;
- **Richard II (r. 1377–1399)**: under the pressures after the Hundred Years' War (1337–1453) and the Black Death (1348–1349), he tried to centralize and was overthrown by the nobles;
- **Wars of the Roses (1455–1485)**: the houses of Lancaster and York fought a civil war; after Bosworth Field in 1485, Henry VII ended it through a dynastic marriage and opened the Tudor period;
- **Tudors (1485–1603)**: the social contradiction shifted from crown versus nobility to Protestant versus Catholic (nobility versus church). Henry VIII (r. 1509–1547), lacking an heir and changing wives repeatedly, drove the Reformation through the 1534 Act of Supremacy; after Edward VI (r. 1547–1553), Bloody Mary (Mary I, r. 1553–1558) brought Catholicism back to the throne, and Lady Jane Grey, the nine-day queen (1553), was executed; under Elizabeth I (r. 1558–1603) Protestantism settled, the privateer era began, and the Spanish Armada was defeated in 1588. Elizabeth had no heir, so in 1603 James I of the Stuart dynasty (r. 1603–1625) arrived from Scotland;
- **Civil War and Restoration (1625–1688)**: Charles I (r. 1625–1649) pushed absolutism; his court painter was Van Dyck, Rubens' pupil and assistant. While curbing the nobles, the English Civil War broke out (1642–1651); the nobles won, Charles I was executed in 1649, and Cromwell became Lord Protector before sliding into dictatorship. The Restoration followed under Charles II (r. 1660–1685), amid the Great Plague of 1665 and the Great Fire of London in 1666. Under James II (r. 1685–1688), the Parliament–king conflict tangled together Catholicism on the king's side and Protestantism on Parliament's side, and escalated;
- **Glorious Revolution (1688–1689)**: Parliament brought in Mary II and William III, and the 1689 Bill of Rights established parliamentary sovereignty; the modern capitalist state took shape.

## Closing

The three paths share the same state machinery—army, taxation, bureaucracy—backed by surplus output and productivity. They differ in who monopolizes violence and where sovereignty sits. The lesson of English history is that strength determines rules and ideas: rules and ideas do not grow out of thin air; behind them lies the balance of power.
