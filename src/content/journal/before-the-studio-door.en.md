## 1. Romanticism in the Statue of Liberty

Begin with a statue. The Statue of Liberty was a gift from France to the United States, and her face and bearing trace back to Delacroix's *Liberty Leading the People*. Two things in that painting stay with you: the goddess's armpit hair, and the colors rolling and flying across the canvas.

The bond between Romanticism and the French Revolution runs deep: the Revolution was a social upheaval charged with instantaneous emotional force and movement, and Romanticism is the reflection of that spirit. What it wanted was not settled order, but passion bursting forth in a flash, and momentum in full flood.

## 2. Tensions Among the Schools

Look at the Paris art world through a lens of contradiction and negation of the negation, and each school falls into place.

Across from Romanticism stands the Academy: Neoclassicism around Ingres, its brushwork serving the taste and imagination of the aristocracy—refined, decorous, painting nonetheless for salons and chambers.

In intellectual opposition to Romanticism lies Realism. Courbet and his circle painted the poor, the everyday things dismissed as "vulgar"; he painted body hair too, but not an idealized armpit hair, not a revolutionary one—only *The Origin of the World*: a matter-of-fact, non-idealized vision.

## 3. Manet: A Second Negation of the Academy

Following the dialectic onward, the second negation of the Academy arrives with Manet's pre-Impressionism, the eve of Impressionism.

Manet placed bourgeois reality inside mythic subjects: *Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe* and *Olympia* drape the petite-bourgeois mood and the modern capitalist condition in a classical veil. The lamp he lit for those who came after was one of Impressionism's defining spirits: leave the studio.

## 4. Air and Sunlight

Outside the studio lie two things it cannot supply: air, and sunlight.

Air carries an enchanting color—figures and landscapes hang in a visible medium, their tones shifting with depth and texture. Sunlight loosens traditional perspective, flattens the picture, and fixes light and shadow into single instants.

## 5. A New Kind of Truth

Why Impressionism came to dominate the salons goes back to truth. Yet truth means different things: it can be the medieval truth the Pre-Raphaelites longed to revive, or the new truth the eye produces when it integrates color for itself.

The eye does not see the world through studio fantasy; it reads color through air and against sunlight. What Impressionism discarded was the ideal invented by Neoclassicism; in its place came the vision generated on the spot, under the light.

## 6. Rivers Meeting: The Perspective Called "the Instant"

By this stage the streams converge: the Barbizon painters sketching in the woods of Fontainebleau, Manet's modern air, Constable's tranquil pastoral, Turner's charged movement and expressivity; alongside Japanese ukiyo-e—diagonal composition, a distinctive vanishing point, an on-site immediacy—and a film-like cropping full of movement.

Fused together, these elements yield a new perspective in time and space. Its name is the instant: the Impressionist instant.

## Closing

Impressionism did not descend from nowhere. Romanticism lit its passion, Realism corrected its gaze, and Manet pushed open the door; once painters stepped out of the studio and took hold of air and sunlight, the age of the instant unfolded.
