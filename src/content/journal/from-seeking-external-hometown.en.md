*Written for ENGL2000J Academic Writing, UM-SJTU Joint Institute, Spring 2025.*

“The Hawk” by Jules Chung is a short story depicting how a baby hawk transforms an Asian American mother. The mother undergoes a transition from searching for an imaginary, external belonging to creating a concrete, intimate relationship with her daughter. As an Asian American, the mother struggles with the sense of being a perpetual foreigner. Before encountering the hawk, she keeps seeking an abstract place of belonging for her family by forcing the daughter to go to church, which deteriorates her relationship with her daughter. However, during the interaction with the hawk, she constructs an imaginary relationship with the baby hawk, which prompts her deep self-reflection. At last, by letting go of the baby hawk, she realizes she should give up the fantasy for an abstract hometown and take her daughter not to the church, but to a concrete, healthy family relation where they can call home.

As an Asian American, the mother suffers from the feelings of perpetual foreigners. The ambiguity in their ethnic identity is an inherent conflict, which complicates their affirmation and belonging to ethnic groups. Belonging in this essay refers to a place where one can gain respect and emotional security. Specifically, respect means caring what one truly needs, while emotional security is an environment without threat and exclusion. According to Professor Yang’s slides, Asian Americans, in search of belonging, are subject to “a push to maintain their cultural and ethnic distinctions” and “a pull to gain inclusion amongst the white American mainstream” (Yang, 21 Feb 2025). However, in America, though placing Asian Americans as “almost white” (Yang, 25 Feb 2025), the white “ostracizes” them as “forever foreigners” (Park 2375). The feelings of perpetual foreigners intensify their longing for belonging. They deem themselves as “guests” in the United States who “must live by the hosts’ rules” (Ogbu 1989 in Lee 413) and internalize this stereotype as a norm white people set. Their “over-anxious attempts” to gain acceptance have “stripped them of dignity” and “caused them to suffer from psychological disorders” (Suzuki 1977).

Feeling like a perpetual foreigner, the mother fixates on seeking a place of belonging for her family, which strains the relationship with her daughter. Her obsession with church is because her Korean spiritual hometown has gone. Driven by “a push” to maintain her Korean culture and ethnicity (Yang), she reminisces about the feeling that the church gave to her in her childhood. By recollecting the “amazing food at picnic” and the weekly “glees from seeing Korean friends”, the mother glorifies her childhood memory in the Korean community, which constitutes her spiritual hometown (Chung). Nevertheless, the churches in which their generation grew up have disappeared, and “the children of that community have moved on, scattered” (Chung). The Korean church nowadays, which is completely different from previous ones, gives her an “uncomfortable throwback”, reminding her of her mental displacement (Chung). Given the absence of the Korean hometown, the mother is pulled to “gain inclusion amongst the American mainstream” (Yang). Therefore, she forces the Western religion on herself and her daughter, hoping religion can grant her the identity as “white” (Yang). However, they are still “ostracized” as “forever foreigners” (Park 2375) because of the family’s apathy to the religion, as she and her daughter both deem the church as a “working conference” without “meaningful rituals” (Chung). As a result, due to the loss of her Korean ethnicity and the failure to gain integration into the white culture, the family can hardly find a community “where they feel fit” (Chung). This sense of homelessness intensifies her eagerness for identity and belonging, which traps her in the “over-anxious attempts” to “gain acceptance” by the Western religious culture (Suzuki 1977). Furthermore, the mother’s persistence on religion leads to the daughter’s resistance and anger towards her, since the daughter becomes “inexplicably angry, sometimes explosive” every time the mother talks about religious stuff (Chung). In summary, her obsession with her imaginary belonging not only yields no sense of belonging but also undermines real family bonds.

Her transition in thoughts takes place when the emergence of the baby hawk offers her an imaginary relationship that temporarily relieves her loneliness. In this imaginary relationship, she assumes the role of the mother hawk. This act happens subconsciously. She is not yet aware that she is beginning to act as a provider of belonging rather than a seeker of it, but her awareness evolves through her action. The hawk’s identity as a flying bird without permanent residence implicates her ethnic history of immigration and displacement as “guests in the United States” (Ogbu 1989 in Lee 413). Therefore, in the baby hawk lost in the snow, injured, she sees her helplessness and loneliness as a perpetual foreigner. By taking on the motherly figure, she gives the baby hawk the sense of belonging she herself craves. Firstly, by “speaking softly and almost not at all” and “poising it on my lap like a delicate football”, she keeps from terrifying the fragile baby, offering it emotional security (Chung). Secondly, she perceives the hawk as “wild and worthy of respect” and deliberately refrains from petting it, demonstrating her reverence (Chung). This fulfills her self-respect, which Asian Americans, “stripped of dignity to gain acceptance”, yearn for (Suzuki 1977). In this imaginary relationship, she discovers her sense of belonging through fostering it in others, which challenges the notion that belonging must be granted by others. Her awakening occurs when the mother calls out, "Junior! Where are you?" to invite the daughter into a joyful role-play (Chung). The long-absent laughter between them temporarily bridges the mother-daughter divide and brings back the feelings of “old times”, the past closeness associated with belonging (Chung). This nostalgic flashback reminds her that a healthy family interaction without the imposition of values can cure the family’s displacement, and she can actively cultivate that for her daughter. Therefore, this imaginary relationship leads to self-reflection and transformation in the mother’s attitude towards her daughter and her own identity.

At last, by releasing the baby hawk, the mother experiences a profound realization that true belonging is not found in an abstract, imagined homeland but in the relationship that she must actively create with real people. When the baby hawk is taken back to the same spot, the mother looks up and sees “the circling mother hawk has gone” (Chung). At this moment, she realizes that the Korean spiritual homeland of her childhood has faded like the mother hawk. For years, she has clung to the fantasy that the Western culture, particularly through religious institutions, will welcome her family like a motherly figure that she acts as in the minivan. Yet, it still “ostracizes” them as “forever foreigners” (Park 2375), just as the internet advises “they should have left the baby hawk where it was” (Chung). Her revelation deepens when she tells her daughter, “The mother won’t come back if we stay” (Chung). She recognizes that if they fixate on an imaginary relationship with the Western religion, the sense of belonging they felt in childhood and long for now will never return. Instead, just like the baby hawk leaves the minivan and “unfolds her outsize wings and takes a step” in the snow, “wobbly” as she is, they must forsake this illusion of home and actively construct their own identity and belonging (Chung). From the previous role-play, she finds out that her identity should be a mother and her belonging should stem from the family interaction. Previously, she is “haunted by what the daughter thinks about her someday”, daunted by the daughter’s “scars”, and frustrated by her impotence to help her daughter with “her brawl inside” as a perpetual foreigner (Chung). This is because she fails to assume the responsibility of a true mother who creates a sense of belonging for her daughter. She neither provides her daughter with emotional security in the exclusive white culture nor respects her daughter’s thoughts, imposing her own religious needs on her daughter. As a result, she not only loses her daughter’s reverence for her but is also rendered mentally unstable by constant self-blame regarding the mother-daughter relationship, which exacerbates her problems for belonging. Now, she strives to be a genuine mother who prioritizes her daughter’s true needs over her own imagination. This, in turn, solves her own “psychological disorders” because in creating a sense of belonging for her daughter, she simultaneously roots herself in her new identity as a mother rather an Asian American (Suzuki 1977). When her daughter asks, “So I guess we are going to church now?”, the mother replies, “Let’s just get lunch” (Chung). This brief exchange carries profound significance. The church symbolizes her past pursuit of illusory belonging, an abstract promise of unconditional acceptance, while Lunch represents a shift toward concrete, real-world mother-daughter interactions. By choosing to share a meal, she anchors belonging in daily acts of care rather than abstract institutional promises. As “a stream of light splits the sky”, this image indicates that they find their own light in the relationship they choose to build.

In conclusion, the mother’s transformation in “The Hawk” reflects a journey from seeking an imagined, abstract place of belonging to actively constructing a concrete sense of home through her relationship with her daughter. Initially, her fixation on finding a spiritual homeland, whether through nostalgic memories of her Korean church or attempts to assimilate into Western religious culture, only deepens her feelings of displacement and strains her family bond. However, her encounter with the baby hawk prompts her to realize that true belonging is not something passively granted but something actively nurtured. By offering the hawk emotional security and respect, she unwittingly discovers how to foster belonging within her own family. Ultimately, her decision to forgo the church visit in favor of sharing a simple lunch with her daughter symbolizes this shift, a conscious move from abstract ideals to tangible connections. This narrative reveals that the struggles of Asian American’s perpetual foreigner dilemma cannot be resolved through imaginary cultural identification alone; instead, true belonging is built through authentic relationships and meaningful human interaction.
